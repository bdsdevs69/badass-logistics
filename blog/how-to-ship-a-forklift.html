How to Ship a Forklift: Rigging and Load-Out Guide

A forklift is not cargo you strap down and forget. Get the mast position, the tie-down points and the power source wrong and it arrives damaged, leaking, or dead on the trailer.

Machinery · By the Badass Logistics crew · September 22, 2026

// quick answer

Shipping a forklift means lowering the forks flat and locking the mast, disconnecting the battery or shutting the fuel valve, and chaining through structural tie-down points — the frame, the counterweight and the drive axle housing, never the mast, the forks or the overhead guard. Load from a matched-height dock where possible, or a rated ramp sized for the truck's counterweight where there is no dock.

A forklift ships wrong more often than most equipment because it looks simple. It has wheels, it drives itself onto the trailer, and it looks like nothing more than a heavy pallet jack. None of that is true once you look at what's actually moving: several thousand pounds of counterweight sitting behind a mast that can free-fall if the hydraulics aren't locked, a battery or a fuel tank that can leak or arc if it's handled wrong, and a set of forks that are not, and were never designed to be, a tie-down point. The trucks that show up to a dock with a damaged mast or a battery casing cracked open almost always got there because someone treated the load-out as a driving job instead of a rigging job.

How do you prepare a forklift before it's loaded?

Lower the forks flat to the deck or the trailer bed, tilt the mast fully forward or to its neutral position per the manufacturer's transport instructions, and engage whatever mast lock or pin the truck has for travel. A forklift's mast rides on hydraulic cylinders, and hydraulic pressure bleeds down over time — a mast that's raised or tilted back when the truck starts moving can drift or drop under its own weight during transit, and a mast that drops onto a trailer's headboard or another piece of freight does real damage to both. Most trucks built for regular transport have a mast tilt lock or a pin specifically for this; if the unit doesn't, block the mast mechanically so it can't move regardless of what the hydraulics do.

Set the parking brake, chock the wheels before anything else touches the truck, and remove or secure anything loose in the operator's compartment — a fire extinguisher, a clipboard, a spare fork extension — that can become a projectile on the road. If the forklift has a removable overhead guard or attachment (a fork positioner, a side-shift carriage, a boom), confirm with the shipper whether it travels mounted or crated separately; an attachment left loose on the mast is one more thing swinging on the same hydraulics that are supposed to be locked down.

How do you handle the battery on an electric forklift before shipping?

Disconnect the battery at the connector, not just switch the key off, and secure the cables so they can't chafe, ground out, or get caught in the mast during loading. A lead-acid forklift battery is a sealed but not indestructible case full of electrolyte sitting on rollers or a slide-out tray, and it stays live as long as it's connected — a short across the terminals during transit, from a loose cable or a stray tool, can arc, heat the case, and in the worst case vent gas or acid. Disconnecting it is the single cheapest step in the whole load-out and the one most often skipped because the truck "still runs fine" with it connected.

Check that the battery retention — the bar, strap, or door that keeps it seated in its compartment during normal operation — is actually latched, not just resting closed. A battery that shifts in its tray under braking or cornering can pinch cables or crack terminal posts even if the truck itself is chained down perfectly. If the battery is being shipped separately from the truck, which happens on longer moves or when a battery is being swapped at the destination, it needs its own upright, secured position — never laid on its side, never stacked under other freight, and terminals capped or taped so nothing metal can bridge them in transit.

How do you handle fuel and gas on an IC forklift before shipping?

Shut the fuel valve — the LP cylinder valve on a propane truck, or the fuel shutoff on a diesel or gasoline unit — and confirm there's no active leak at the connection before the truck goes anywhere near the trailer. A propane-powered forklift almost always ships with the LP cylinder still mounted on the counterweight bracket at the rear; the valve closes, the cylinder stays secured in its own mounting cage exactly the way it rides during normal operation, and that cage is checked separately from the truck's own tie-downs since it's carrying a pressurized cylinder, not dead weight.

On diesel and gas units, top off is not required and running the tank close to empty is the more common practice for shipping — less fuel is less weight, less spill risk if a line works loose, and less to deal with if the unit sits for a while before it's put back into service. Whatever's left in the tank, check hose connections and the fuel cap before the truck rolls, since vibration over distance finds a loose fitting a short forklift ride around the yard never would.

What has to be confirmed before a forklift is loaded

  • Forks flat, mast tilted forward or neutral, and mechanically locked or pinned against drift
  • Parking brake set and wheels chocked before the truck touches the trailer
  • Electric: battery disconnected at the connector, retention latched, cables secured away from moving parts
  • LP/gas/diesel: fuel valve closed, cylinder or tank cage checked, no active leak at the connection
  • Attachments — side-shift, positioner, boom — confirmed as ship-mounted or removed and crated separately
  • Weight and axle distribution known before choosing tie-down points, not assumed from the nameplate

Where do you tie down a forklift for transport?

Chain through the truck's frame — the frame rails, the counterweight, the drive axle housing, or the purpose-built tie-down lugs the manufacturer welds on for exactly this — never through the mast, the forks, the overhead guard, the hydraulic lines, or anything bolted rather than structural. The mast belongs on the same banned list as the forks: it is a lifting assembly built to take vertical load in its own channels, not the lateral and fore-aft loads a tie-down puts into it, and a chain pulled across it racks the very thing you spent the load-out protecting. Forks are a lever arm hanging off the front of the truck; a chain across them under load transfers force into the carriage and lift chains, not into the frame, and can bend or misalign the whole mast assembly long before it fails outright. The overhead guard is sheet steel and tube designed to stop falling debris, not to take a highway-speed load transfer sideways.

Most forklifts built for regular over-the-road shipping have tie-down points cast or welded into the frame specifically for this — check the operator's manual or the data plate area for them before improvising. Where there's no obvious factory point, the axle housings themselves are usually the safest structural anchor on both ends of the truck. Chain angle matters as much as the anchor point: a chain running nearly vertical from a low frame point to a high trailer stake pocket does little to stop the truck sliding forward under braking, where most of the securement load actually falls on a road trip.

Because a forklift is a wheeled, self-propelled load, it gets treated under the same securement logic as any other vehicle being transported — direct tie-downs at structural points, rated for the truck's actual weight.

Why does the counterweight matter for how you load a forklift?

Because it moves the truck's real center of gravity toward the rear axle, which changes how it behaves on a ramp and how it has to sit on a trailer deck. A forklift is built to carry its heaviest point behind the front wheels on purpose — that's what lets it lift a load out front without tipping — but the same design means driving it up or down an incline puts it closer to its tipping threshold than a vehicle with evenly distributed weight. Drive a loaded truck nose-first up a steep ramp and the combination of the ramp angle and the rear-heavy counterweight can lift the front wheels or reduce steering traction right when control matters most.

On the trailer itself, position the forklift so its weight lands where the deck and the truck's own axles are rated to carry it — generally centered fore-and-aft relative to the trailer's axle group, not shoved to one end because that's where it fit. A forklift positioned too far to the rear can overload the trailer's rear axles and lighten the tractor's steer axle; too far forward does the opposite. This is arranged before the truck ever drives up the ramp, not corrected after it's already chained down.

Ramp, dock, or forklift-on-forklift — how do you decide?

A dock that matches the trailer bed height is the easiest and safest load-out: the forklift drives straight across at a level transition with no incline to manage and no ramp geometry to check. Where that's available, it's the default. Most warehouse and yard load-outs run this way because the dock height and the trailer height are built to match within a few inches.

Where there's no dock, a rated ramp bridges the gap — but it has to be rated for the forklift's actual weight, not its advertised capacity under ideal conditions, and set at an angle the truck's ground clearance and counterweight can handle. Steep, short ramps concentrate the angle change right at the ramp-to-bed transition, exactly where a rear-heavy machine is most likely to lift its front end or bottom out. A long, shallow ramp beats a short, steep one every time the site allows it.

Forklift-on-forklift — loading one truck onto a trailer using a second, larger forklift to lift it — is a real option on smaller units, but it's a rigging pick, not a driving maneuver, and it only works when the lifting truck's derated capacity clears the loaded truck's actual weight with real margin, the same load-center math that governs any machine pick. It also needs sound pick points on the truck being loaded — most forklifts aren't built with fork pockets for being lifted the way a crated machine is, so this method usually means chains or straps around the frame and axle rather than forks under the load, and it's a call worth making with a rigging crew rather than improvising on site.

What has to be confirmed when a forklift is received at the other end?

Check the load before the chains come off — mast still locked, tires and wheels intact, no visible fluid under the truck, battery or fuel connections undisturbed. Once the chains are released, an unsecured mast or a truck that shifted in transit can move unexpectedly, so a visual check before anything is unchained catches a problem while it's still contained instead of after the forklift is rolling off the trailer.

Reconnect the battery or reopen the fuel valve only after checking for a cracked case, a loose terminal, or a fuel line that worked itself off a fitting. Start the truck at idle, run the mast through its full range with nothing on the forks to confirm the hydraulics still hold, and check that the lock or pin released cleanly before it goes back to work.

Bottom line

  • Forks down, mast locked, brake set and chocked — before a single tie-down chain goes on.
  • Electric trucks ship with the battery disconnected at the connector; IC trucks ship with the fuel valve closed and the connection checked for leaks.
  • Chain through the frame — frame rails, counterweight, axle housings, or factory tie-down lugs — never the mast, forks, overhead guard, or hydraulic lines.
  • The counterweight sets the real center of gravity; it decides ramp angle, position on the trailer, and whether forklift-on-forklift is even an option.
  • A matched-height dock beats a ramp, and a long shallow ramp beats a short steep one.
  • Check the truck before the chains come off at the other end, and run the mast and hydraulics empty before it goes back to work.

That's the rigging side of a forklift move — the part that happens before and after the truck is on the trailer. Frequently asked questions

How do you prepare a forklift for shipping?
Lower the forks flat, tilt the mast forward or to neutral and lock or pin it against drift, set the parking brake, and chock the wheels before anything else touches the trailer. Electric trucks get the battery disconnected at the connector; IC trucks get the fuel or LP valve closed and the connection checked for leaks. Loose attachments and anything in the operator compartment get secured or removed before the truck is loaded.
Where do you tie down a forklift on a trailer?
Through structural points — the frame rails, the counterweight, the drive axle housing, or factory tie-down lugs where the truck has them — never through the mast, the forks, the overhead guard, or hydraulic lines. The mast and forks are a lifting assembly built for vertical load, not an anchor: a chain pulled across either one puts lateral and fore-aft force through the carriage and lift chains and can bend or misalign the mast under road load.
Do you need to disconnect the battery to ship an electric forklift?
Yes. Disconnect it at the connector, not just power the truck down, so a loose cable or stray tool can't short across live terminals during transit. Confirm the battery retention bar or strap is actually latched, since a battery that shifts in its tray can pinch cables or crack terminals even on a truck that's chained down correctly.
Can you load a forklift with a ramp instead of a dock?
Yes, as long as the ramp is rated for the forklift's actual weight and set at an angle the truck's ground clearance and rear-heavy counterweight can handle without the front end lifting or the undercarriage bottoming out at the transition point. A dock that matches the trailer height removes the incline problem entirely and is the safer default where one is available.
Can one forklift load another forklift onto a trailer?
On smaller units, yes, but it's a rigging pick, not a driving maneuver — it only works when the lifting truck's derated capacity clears the loaded truck's actual weight with real margin, and it usually means chaining around the frame and axle rather than forking under the truck, since most forklifts aren't built with pockets for being lifted the way a crated machine is.
What should you check when a forklift arrives at its destination?
Before releasing the chains, check that the mast is still locked, the tires and wheels are intact, and there's no fluid visible under the truck. After unchaining, reconnect the battery or reopen the fuel valve, then run the mast through its full range empty and start the truck at idle to confirm the hydraulics and tires came through the move undamaged before it goes back to work.
