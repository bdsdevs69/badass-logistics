Truck Dispatch Services for Small Fleets (4+ Trucks): What to Expect

A dispatch service built for owner-operators and one built for fleets are different animals. Here is what a fleet should expect and ask.

FIELD GUIDE BY THE CREW ✓

Dispatch · By the Badass Logistics crew · September 17, 2026

// quick answer

A fleet dispatch service finds and books freight, plans reloads across trucks, negotiates rates, and runs the back office — carrier setups, rate confirmations, check calls, detention claims, and billing paperwork. For fleets of roughly 4 to 20 trucks, outsourcing can cost less than building an in-house desk and bring freight sources a small carrier can't reach alone. Before signing, ask about forced dispatch, where freight comes from, exactly what back-office work is included, how pricing works, and how to exit the contract.

At one truck, the owner is the dispatcher. At fifty trucks, a company has a dispatch department. In between — somewhere around four to twenty trucks — is the stretch where dispatch starts eating the owner's day but a full in-house desk doesn't pencil out yet. That's where fleet dispatch services come in, and where picking the wrong one hurts the most.

What does a fleet dispatch service actually do?

Finding loads is the part everyone thinks of. It's maybe a third of the job. A real dispatch service for fleets covers:

  • Freight sourcing — direct shippers, brokers, project freight, and load boards, matched to each truck's equipment and lane.
  • Lane and reload planning — lining up the next load before a truck empties, across the whole fleet.
  • Rate negotiation — pushing each load toward what the lane and equipment are worth.
  • Setup and rate cons — carrier packets, broker and shipper setups, and reviewing rate confirmation terms.
  • In-transit follow-through — check calls, tracking updates, appointment changes.
  • Accessorials — documenting and pursuing detention, layover, and truck-ordered-not-used.
  • Paperwork — BOLs and PODs collected, invoice packets built, factoring submissions sent.
  • Hours and home time — planning around each driver's available hours and commitments.

Outsourced dispatch vs an in-house dispatcher — which fits a small fleet?

The tradeoff

  • In-house dispatcher — full control and company focus, but you carry salary, turnover, coverage for nights and days off, and the dispatcher's personal freight network is your ceiling.
  • Outsourced fleet dispatch — coverage and back office without the hire, plus access to the service's freight relationships. You give up some day-to-day control and need clear agreements.

For many fleets in the four-to-twenty range, outsourced dispatch covers the hours a single hire can't and brings freight sources that are hard for a small carrier to build alone. Bigger fleets often move dispatch in-house once the volume supports a team.

Why do some dispatch services only work with fleets?

Dispatching one truck and dispatching a fleet are different jobs. With several trucks on the same desk, reloads can be traded between trucks, lanes can be planned instead of chased, and a steady freight source can be kept covered week after week. That's why some services — ours included — set a minimum fleet size. We dispatch trucking companies with at least four power units and don't take owner-operators, because the desk is built around planning lanes across a fleet and covering project freight from our own network.

What should you ask a dispatch company before signing?

  1. Is it forced dispatch? You should approve every load. A service that books without your approval is making decisions only you should make.
  2. Who do they work for? A dispatcher should act for your company. Ask whether they receive compensation from brokers or shippers for the loads they book — and read up on dispatcher vs freight broker roles.
  3. Where does the freight come from? Only load boards, or direct shippers and steady project freight too?
  4. What exactly is included? Setups, check calls, detention claims, billing, factoring submissions — get the list in writing.
  5. How is pricing structured? Models vary — commonly a percentage of revenue or a flat fee per truck. Understand what's included and whether anything is billed extra.
  6. Who has access to what? Load board accounts, factoring portals, email — know who controls each.
  7. How do you exit? Notice period, return of documents, and what happens to loads in progress.

What are the red flags?

  • Guaranteed weekly revenue numbers before they know your lanes or equipment.
  • Pressure to run every load offered, or penalties for declining loads.
  • Vague answers about who pays them besides you.
  • No written agreement, or one that makes leaving difficult.
  • No clear process for detention, TONU, and paperwork — just "we find loads."
  • Requests for control of your authority or accounts beyond what the work requires.

Bottom line

  • Fleet dispatch is freight plus back office, planned across trucks.
  • For roughly 4 to 20 trucks, outsourcing often beats building a desk.
  • Get approval rights, freight sources, scope, pricing, and exit terms in writing.

Frequently asked questions

How many trucks do you need to use a fleet dispatch service?
It depends on the service. Some take single trucks; fleet-focused services often set a minimum. Badass Logistics dispatches trucking companies with four or more power units.
Is outsourced dispatch cheaper than hiring a dispatcher?
For many small fleets it can be, once salary, coverage for nights and days off, and back-office work are counted. It depends on fleet size, freight, and what the service includes.
What is forced dispatch?
When a dispatcher books loads without the carrier's approval or penalizes the carrier for declining. Carriers should approve every load.
How do truck dispatch services charge?
Common models are a percentage of load revenue or a flat fee per truck. Always confirm what is included and whether any services are billed separately.
Does a dispatcher need broker authority?
A dispatcher acting only for the carrier is different from a broker arranging freight for shippers. The rules depend on who pays the dispatcher and how they operate, so review FMCSA guidance or get legal advice for your situation.
