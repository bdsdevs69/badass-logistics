Crane Rental vs Rigging Company: Which Do You Need?

Renting a crane and hiring a rigging crew are not competing quotes. They are different scopes, and the gap between them is where jobs go wrong.

Rigging · By the Badass Logistics crew · September 18, 2026

// quick answer

Crane rental supplies a machine, usually with an operator, and the customer stays responsible for the lift plan, the rigging hardware, the signal person and everything that happens once the load leaves the hook. A rigging company owns the whole pick — survey, plan, gear, crew, and moving and setting the load. Rent a crane when you already have qualified riggers. Hire a rigger when you do not.

Two quotes come back for the same job and they are nowhere near each other. One is a crane with an operator for the day. The other is a rigging company's number for the whole lift. The instinct is to treat the cheaper one as the same thing with less padding, and that is the mistake — they are not quoting the same work. One of them is renting you a machine. The other is taking responsibility for getting your load from where it is to where it needs to be, in one piece.

Both are correct answers. They are correct for different jobs.

What does crane rental actually include?

There are two versions of it, and the difference matters more than anything else on this page.

  • Bare rental. The machine only. You supply the operator, and in doing so you become the operating employer — the qualification of the person in the seat, the inspections, the lift planning and the supervision all sit with you. Outside of contractors who run their own iron, this is rarely what anyone wants.
  • Operated and maintained rental. The common one. Machine, an operator qualified and certified for that crane type, fuel, maintenance and mobilization to site. The operator runs the crane and refuses a lift that is unsafe — which is not the same as planning it.

What operated rental normally does not include: the engineered lift plan, the rigging hardware between the hook and your load, riggers to attach it, a qualified signal person, ground preparation and mats, permits or street closures, and any movement of the load that is not a straight pick and set. The crane leaves when the pick is done. If your machine is sitting in the parking lot on dunnage and still has to get through a door and onto a foundation, that is not in the rental.

What does a rigging company do differently?

It owns the outcome rather than a machine-hour. A rigging scope normally starts before anything arrives and ends when the load is where it belongs:

  1. The site survey. Weights and center of gravity, pick points, crane position, ground bearing, swing radius, overhead obstructions, and the route the load takes after it lands.
  2. The lift plan. Load chart against radius, configuration, sling angles and tensions, rigging capacity with the right reductions, and who is the lift director.
  3. The rigging gear. Slings, shackles, spreader or lifting beam, softeners, tag lines — sized, inspected and documented for that lift.
  4. The crew. Riggers on the load, a designated signal person, a lift director running the pick.
  5. Everything after the hook. Jacks, skates, rollers, gantry, forklift — moving the load inside, then setting, leveling and anchoring it.

The crane, in that model, is one tool among several. A good rigging company will happily tell you that your job does not need a crane at all — that a gantry over the top or a jack-and-skid through the door is faster and cheaper — which is advice no one is incentivized to give you when they are renting out the machine.

Who is responsible for the lift?

This is the question worth settling in writing before anyone mobilizes, because it is the one that gets assumed. On an operated rental, the crane owner is responsible for the crane, its maintenance, its inspections and its operator's qualification. The user of the crane is responsible for the lift itself — the plan, the ground it sits on, the rigging, the signals and the load. On a bare rental, all of it lands on you. With a rigging company running the pick, one party holds the plan, the gear, the crew and the load, and there is nobody to point at.

Insurance follows the same line. A crane rental's coverage protects the crane. Damage to the thing hanging underneath it is a different policy — riggers liability, or a care, custody and control endorsement — and if the load is a machine worth more than the building it is going into, that distinction is the whole conversation. Ask for the certificate, and ask what it covers, not just that it exists.

When is crane rental the right call?

When you already have the rest of the scope covered in-house. Specifically:

  • You have qualified riggers and a competent lift director on staff — steel erectors, mechanical contractors and large plant maintenance groups usually do.
  • The lift is repetitive and well understood: setting rooftop units, feeding materials up a building, the same pick you did last month.
  • The load is simple, forgiving and has obvious pick points.
  • You need reach or capacity for a few hours and nothing else.
  • You are controlling the site anyway and the crane is one trade among many.

When do you want a rigging company instead?

  • The load is a machine — anything that has to land on a foundation and then be leveled and aligned to a tolerance. Setting it down is the easy half.
  • The load does not stop at the hook. If it has to go through a door, down an aisle, up a ramp or into a mezzanine, somebody has to move it after the crane leaves.
  • It is a critical lift — near capacity, over occupied space, multi-crane, or a load whose value or irreplaceability makes a drop unthinkable.
  • The pick points are not obvious, or the manufacturer has specific ones and the gear has to respect them.
  • You want one accountable party. When the rigging, the crane and the setting are three vendors, the gap between them is where the damage happens and where the argument starts.
  • Nobody on your side wants to sign the lift plan. That is a completely reasonable position, and it is the clearest signal that this is a rigging job.

Ask before you accept either quote

  • Who writes and signs the lift plan, and who is the designated lift director?
  • Who supplies and inspects the slings, shackles and spreader?
  • Who provides the signal person and the riggers on the load?
  • Who verifies ground bearing, and who supplies outrigger mats?
  • What happens to the load after it is set down — and is that in the price?
  • What covers damage to the load itself, not just to the crane?
  • Who pulls permits, handles street or lane closure, and notifies the facility?

Does the job even need a crane?

Often not, and that is worth asking early. A crane is the answer when the load has to go up and over something — a wall, a building, a fence line, another machine — or when the reach is genuinely beyond what can be done from the ground. Inside a building, a gantry, a forklift within its derated capacity, or jacks and skates will usually do the same work with less setup, less weather risk and no outrigger loading on a slab that was never designed for it. The decision between a forklift and a pick is its own comparison — and the load-center derating on a forklift load-out is where most of the surprises live.

It also cuts the other way. Rigging crews that never reach for a crane will occasionally spend a day skidding something that a one-hour pick would have solved. The point is not that one tool is better; it is that the choice should come out of a survey rather than out of whatever the vendor happens to own.

Can you hire both?

Yes, and on larger jobs it is normal. The rigging company runs the lift and brings in an operated crane rental as part of its plan, which keeps the lift plan, the gear and the crew with one party while the machine comes from whoever has the right capacity available that week. What you want to avoid is the version where you contract the crane yourself, contract riggers separately, and end up as the general contractor of a lift you did not plan. If that is how the job is shaping up, put one party in charge of the pick.

Bottom line

  • Operated crane rental buys a machine and an operator, not a lift plan, rigging gear or riggers.
  • A rigging company owns the whole pick, including everything that happens after the hook.
  • Rent a crane when you have qualified riggers and a lift director already. Otherwise hire the rigger.
  • Settle who plans the lift, who supplies the gear and what covers the load — in writing, before mobilization.
  • Ask whether a crane is needed at all. Inside a building it frequently is not.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between crane rental and hiring a rigging company?
Crane rental supplies a machine, and in the operated version an operator qualified for that crane type. The customer remains responsible for the lift plan, the rigging hardware, the riggers, the signal person, ground preparation and everything that happens after the load is set down. A rigging company owns the whole pick — survey, lift plan, gear, crew, the lift itself, and then moving, setting and leveling the load.
Does an operated crane rental include the rigging?
Usually not. Operated and maintained rental typically covers the crane, operator, fuel, maintenance and mobilization. Slings, shackles, spreader beams, riggers to attach the load, a designated signal person, outrigger mats, permits and the engineered lift plan are normally separate. Confirm each of those line by line rather than assuming, because that gap is the most common reason a rental quote looks cheaper.
Who is responsible if the load is damaged during a lift?
It depends on the contract, which is why it is worth settling in writing first. A crane rental policy covers the crane; damage to the load underneath it falls under riggers liability or a care, custody and control endorsement, which not every vendor carries. On an operated rental the crane owner is responsible for the crane and its operator while the user is generally responsible for the lift, so ask for the certificate and ask specifically what covers the load.
When should I just rent a crane?
When you already have qualified riggers and a competent lift director on staff, the lift is simple and repetitive, and you only need reach or capacity for a few hours. Steel erectors, mechanical contractors and large plant maintenance groups typically fit that description. If nobody on your side is prepared to sign the lift plan, that is the clearest signal it should be a rigging company’s scope instead.
Do I need a crane to move a machine?
Frequently not. A crane earns its place when the load has to travel up and over something or the reach is beyond what can be done from the ground. Inside a building, a gantry, a forklift within its derated capacity, or jacks and skates usually do the same work with less setup, less weather exposure and no outrigger loading on a slab that was not designed for it. The right answer comes from a site survey.
Can a rigging company supply the crane too?
Yes, and on larger jobs it is the normal arrangement. The rigging company writes and owns the lift plan, supplies the gear and the crew, and brings in an operated crane rental as part of that plan. It keeps responsibility with one party while the machine comes from whoever has the right capacity available. The arrangement to avoid is contracting the crane and the riggers separately and becoming the coordinator of a lift you did not plan.
