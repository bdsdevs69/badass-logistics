How to Evaluate a Truck Dispatch Service for Your Fleet

A sales call tells you what a dispatch service wants you to believe. Here is how to find out what the desk actually does once your trucks are on it.

FIELD GUIDE BY THE CREW ✓
REAL LOADS — REAL NUMBERS NO FLUFF, JUST SPECS FROM THE DISPATCH DESK

Dispatch · By the Badass Logistics crew · October 19, 2026

// quick answer

Evaluate a truck dispatch service by looking past the pitch to the desk itself: how many dispatchers cover your trucks, who backs them up nights and weekends, where freight comes from beyond load boards, and what reporting you get on rates, deadhead, and detention. A real fleet desk has named coverage and numbers it can show you every week — not just a phone that answers once and goes quiet.

Every dispatch service sounds the same on the first call: good freight, fair rates, no forced dispatch, we'll treat your trucks right. None of that tells you anything about what happens three weeks in, when a truck is sitting empty in a town with no freight and the person who signed you up isn't the one answering the phone. Evaluating a dispatch service means testing the things a pitch can't fake — the size and structure of the desk, where the loads actually come from, and whether anyone is tracking the numbers that tell you if the arrangement is working.

What's the difference between a real dispatch desk and one person with a phone?

A real desk has more than one person who can answer for your trucks, and coverage doesn't disappear when one dispatcher is off, sick, or asleep. A one-person operation running dispatch off a cell phone can be fine for a single truck, but it breaks down fast once you're running four or more — there's no backup when that person is on another call, no one covering the lane at 2am when a delivery appointment moves, and no continuity if they quit.

Ask directly: how many dispatchers cover my trucks, and who else can speak to my loads if that person isn't reachable? A legitimate fleet desk can name a second person without hesitating. If the answer is some version of "it's just me, but I'm always on," that's not a desk — that's one person betting they never get sick, never sleep, and never juggle two emergencies at once.

How can you tell where a dispatch service actually gets its freight?

Ask what share of loads comes from load boards versus direct shippers, brokers they have standing relationships with, or their own project freight — and expect a specific answer, not "we have great connections." Load boards aren't bad; they fill gaps. But a service that runs entirely off the boards is re-selling the same spot market your own people could pull up on a phone. What you're paying for is access to freight you couldn't get yourself: direct shipper relationships, a broker network that's actually been vetted, or steady work moving through the service's own operations.

A dispatch desk tied to an active freight or project business has an edge here, because some of what moves through dedicated lanes and ongoing project work needs capacity every week, and fleets on the desk are first in line for it. Ask a prospective service point-blank whether any of the freight they'd offer your trucks comes from their own network rather than a board subscription anyone can buy. If they can't answer, assume the answer is no.

What should you ask to confirm the desk can actually run your fleet?

Beyond the usual questions about forced dispatch and pricing — covered in our guide to dispatch for small fleets — the operational questions are the ones that expose whether a service is built for a fleet or just scaled up from handling single trucks:

  • How many trucks does one dispatcher handle? A dispatcher juggling thirty or forty trucks alone has no time to plan reloads or negotiate rates on any of them well.
  • What happens when two of my trucks could run the same lane? Fleets need loads planned across trucks, not booked truck by truck — ask how they decide which truck gets which load.
  • What system do they run on? A dispatcher working from a notebook and group texts loses track fast once a fleet grows past a handful of trucks. Ask what they use to track loads, rate cons, and paperwork, and whether you get any visibility into it yourself.
  • How do they onboard multiple trucks at once? Setting up four or more trucks — equipment specs, driver hours, home-time needs, existing shipper and broker relationships — takes a real process, not a five-minute call.
  • Can they name two current fleet clients of a similar size? Not a testimonial page — an actual fleet you could call. A service that only dispatches owner-operators and is pitching you as a step up won't have this answer.

What reporting should a fleet get from its dispatcher?

At minimum, a fleet should get a regular rundown of rate per load against the lane and equipment, deadhead miles, detention and TONU logged and pursued, and where each truck stands — not just when you ask, but on a schedule you agreed on going in.

What good reporting looks like

  • Per-load detail — rate, lane, miles, and equipment for every load booked, not a monthly lump sum.
  • Deadhead tracking — how many empty miles each truck ran and why, so you can see whether reloads are actually being planned ahead.
  • Accessorials logged — detention, layover, and TONU tracked and pursued, with a running tally, not forgotten once the load delivers.
  • A standing cadence — daily check calls during transit and a weekly summary at minimum, agreed before the first load moves.

A service that can't produce this isn't necessarily dishonest — it may just not be set up to run fleets yet. Either way, you won't know if your trucks are making money on a lane without it. If pricing structure is still unclear at this point, our breakdown of how dispatch services get paid covers what should be in writing before reporting even becomes relevant.

How do you test a dispatch service before handing over the whole fleet?

Run a portion of the fleet through the service for a few weeks before moving everything over, and judge the result against what was promised on the sales call — not against what sounds reasonable in general. Two or three trucks is enough to see whether reloads get planned ahead of time, whether rates match what was pitched, and whether the person who answers the phone during an actual problem is the one who sold you on the service in the first place.

Watch the first few weeks for specific things: how fast an empty truck gets a reload, whether detention actually gets billed and chased rather than written off, and whether check calls happen on schedule or only when you ask for one. A service that performs well on a small trial and then slips once the whole fleet is committed is a different problem — but testing first at least gives you a baseline to hold them to.

What are the signs a dispatch service isn't actually built for fleets?

  • They'll take any carrier, any size, no minimum — a fleet desk that also dispatches single owner-operators is splitting its attention across two very different jobs.
  • No one can tell you where freight comes from beyond "the boards" and "our network," with no specifics behind the second part.
  • Reporting is whatever you remember to ask for, with no standing format or schedule.
  • No second contact who can speak to your trucks if the one person you've been dealing with is unreachable.
  • They want every truck moved over immediately, with no interest in starting smaller.

Bottom line

  • Judge the desk, not the pitch: named coverage, a second contact, and a real onboarding process for multiple trucks.
  • Make them show where freight actually comes from beyond the load boards.
  • Get reporting on rate, deadhead, and accessorials on a set schedule, and test with part of the fleet first.

Frequently asked questions

How many dispatchers should be covering a fleet of 4 to 10 trucks?
There should be more than one person who can speak to your loads. A single dispatcher handling the fleet alone, with no named backup for nights, weekends, or sick days, is a coverage gap waiting to happen.
What is a reasonable trial period before moving a whole fleet to a dispatch service?
A few weeks with two or three trucks is usually enough to see whether reloads get planned ahead, rates match what was quoted, and reporting actually shows up on schedule before committing the rest of the fleet.
Should a dispatch service explain where its freight comes from?
Yes. A service that can only point to load boards is reselling the same spot market you could access yourself. Ask what share comes from direct shippers, vetted brokers, or freight moving through their own operations.
What reporting should a fleet expect from a dispatcher?
At minimum, per-load rate and lane detail, deadhead tracking, detention and TONU logged and pursued, and a standing schedule for check calls and summaries — agreed before the first load moves, not improvised afterward.
Is it a red flag if a dispatch service also works with owner-operators?
Not automatically, but it means the desk is splitting attention between two different jobs. Dispatching one truck and planning lanes across a fleet require different structure, and a service built for fleets usually sets a minimum truck count for that reason.
