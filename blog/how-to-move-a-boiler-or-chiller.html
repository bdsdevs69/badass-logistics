How to Move an Industrial Boiler or Chiller

Big, heavy, and usually boxed into a mechanical room or a roof screen wall. Here's how boilers and chillers actually get rigged, moved, and set — from the dispatch desk.

Machinery Moving · By the Badass Logistics crew · July 3, 2026 · Updated September 27, 2026

// quick answer

Moving an industrial boiler or chiller is won or lost on access and drain-down, not the truck. Drain the water/glycol side and recover refrigerant through a licensed tech, protect the tube bundle and refractory, rig it out by crane off a roof or on skates through the mechanical room, keep it upright on a flatbed or step deck, then set it level and hand it to the trades for reconnection.

Boilers and chillers are classic rigging problems: heavy machines installed in the worst possible place to get something out of — a basement mechanical room, a penthouse, or a rooftop behind a screen wall. The truck is the easy part. Getting the unit out and the new one in, without damaging a tube bundle or a refractory lining along the way, is the job.

What decides how a boiler or chiller move gets planned?

Before weight or trailer, the first question is how does it get out. A rooftop chiller usually comes off by crane — which means a lift plan, a pick point, and lot or street access for the crane to set up. A basement boiler comes out on skates through corridors and up a ramp or freight elevator, or through a knocked-out wall panel if the original opening has since been built over. Measure the path to the inch: doorways, turns, ceiling height, floor ratings. Many of these units were set before the walls closed in, so the exit is tighter than the entrance ever was.

What has to be drained before a boiler or chiller moves?

Before anything is rigged, the trades drain the water or glycol side, blow the boiler down per code, disconnect electrical, gas, and piping, and cap every line. Chillers hold refrigerant that has to be recovered by a licensed technician before the unit travels — that's a code and environmental requirement, not an option. Getting these disconnects sequenced with the rigging crew ahead of the pick keeps the move from stalling with a crane or trailer sitting idle. On a hot-water or steam boiler, the system also has to cool fully before drain-down starts; draining a hot vessel under pressure is how a crew gets hurt.

Why does the tube bundle or refractory decide how the unit gets rigged?

The shell is not the load path. A shell-and-tube chiller carries its tube bundle suspended inside the shell, and that bundle can shift or stress its tube sheet if the unit is racked, dropped, or lifted from the wrong point — some builders spec an internal brace or shipping bar to hold the bundle still for exactly this reason, and it goes in before the pick and comes out only after the unit is set and leveled. A fire-tube or water-tube boiler carries a refractory lining that's effectively a brittle ceramic shell inside the steel one; it can crack from a hard set-down or from being tipped or racked hard, even when the outside of the vessel looks untouched. Both problems are why lift points matter more than they look like they should: rig from the frame, base rails, or the builder's designated lift lugs, never from piping stubs, control panels, or the shell itself, and keep the unit level through the entire pick rather than correcting a tilt after the fact.

What the site walk has to capture

  • Operating and dry weight, footprint, and the builder's documented lift points or lugs
  • Roof crane access and pick radius, or the skate path — doorways, corridors, ramps, freight elevator capacity
  • Refrigerant charge and type on a chiller, or the boiler's fuel and pressure class, for the disconnect sequence
  • Internal bracing needs — a shipping bar for a tube bundle, or the builder's handling guidance on a refractory-lined boiler
  • Destination mechanical room or roof pad — floor loading, curb height, and clearance for the unit's final orientation
  • Piping, electrical, and controls that the trades will need to reconnect on the other end

How do you decide between a crane pick and a skid-out?

The decision usually isn't a preference — it's forced by where the unit sits. A crane pick makes sense when there's a clear overhead path to the unit: a roof unit behind a screen wall, a chiller in a penthouse mechanical room with a roof hatch or removable panel above it, or a ground-floor unit next to a loading area with room for outriggers. The crane needs a rated pick point, a sling or spreader-bar arrangement sized to the unit's actual weight and center of gravity, and enough clearance on the swing radius to avoid parapets, rooftop equipment, and overhead lines. A skid-out on machinery skates or a low-profile dolly system is the answer when the unit sits below grade, mid-building, or behind a corridor run with no crane access above or beside it — the unit gets jacked up an inch at a time, skates go under the base, and it's walked out slow and centered, with steel plate bridging any floor joint, trench cover, or expansion gap in the path. Some jobs need both: a chiller skated out of a basement mechanical room to a loading dock, then craned onto the trailer once it's clear of the building. The site walk is what settles this, not a rule of thumb — measure the actual overhead clearance and the actual corridor width before committing to either method.

What trailer moves an industrial boiler or chiller?

On the truck, most boilers and chillers ride a flatbed or step deck chosen by weight and height — a tall chiller wants a step deck's lower deck for the clearance. The unit travels upright, blocked and braced at the base, and chained to the frame, never to the shell, coils, or piping stubs. This freight moves through our licensed broker and carrier partners the same way any heavy plant equipment does, matched to a truck with the deck height and axle rating the unit's weight calls for. This is the same rigging and machinery moving discipline as any heavy plant equipment — the chiller or boiler just adds fluid systems and internal fragility to the usual pick-point and load-path problem.

Setting and reconnecting the unit

At the destination the unit is rigged into the mechanical room or onto the roof pad, set level on its supports, and handed back to the trades for piping, electrical, refrigerant charge, and startup. Level matters — a chiller or boiler that isn't sitting square on its supports can strain piping connections and vibration mounts long before anyone notices a problem on a gauge. Any internal bracing installed for transit — a tube-bundle shipping bar, blocked baffles — comes out only after the unit is set and before the trades close it up. On a bigger project, this rigging-in of the mechanical equipment is usually one piece of a broader plant relocation or an HVAC-specific job, covered in more detail on the HVAC and chiller rigging page.

Bottom line

  • Access — roof crane or skate-out through the building — decides the whole plan before weight does.
  • Drain, disconnect, and recover refrigerant (licensed) before the unit moves; let a boiler cool before drain-down.
  • Protect the tube bundle or refractory lining; rig from the frame or lift points, never the shell or piping.
  • Move upright on a flatbed or step deck by weight and height; set level for reconnection.

Frequently asked questions

How do you get a rooftop chiller down?
Usually by crane. That means a lift plan with a rated crane, a clear pick point, and lot or street access for the crane to set up. The chiller is rigged from its frame or lift points, kept level, and lowered to a trailer staged below. Units set below grade or mid-building come out on skates through corridors or a freight elevator instead.
Does the refrigerant have to be removed before moving a chiller?
Yes. Refrigerant must be recovered by a licensed technician before the unit is transported — it's a code and environmental requirement. This is sequenced with the disconnect and rigging work so the move doesn't stall waiting on it.
What protects the tube bundle when a chiller is rigged?
A shell-and-tube chiller's bundle can shift or stress its tube sheet if the unit is racked or dropped, so some builders spec an internal shipping bar or brace that goes in before the pick and comes out only after the unit is set level at the destination. The unit is always rigged from the frame or documented lift points, never from the shell.
Can a boiler be tipped during a move?
Only within the tilt limit the builder rates for the unit, and often not much beyond that. A fire-tube or water-tube boiler's refractory lining is effectively a brittle ceramic shell inside the steel one, and it can crack from a hard set-down or an over-angle tilt without any outside damage showing. The vessel also has to be fully cooled before drain-down, not just depressurized.
What trailer moves an industrial boiler or chiller?
Most ride a flatbed or step deck chosen by weight and height — a step deck's lower deck buys clearance for a tall chiller. The unit travels upright, blocked and braced at the base, and chained to the frame, never to the shell or coils, moved through our licensed broker and carrier partners.
Who handles reconnecting a boiler or chiller after it is set?
Rigging sets the unit level on its supports; the trades then handle piping, electrical, and — on a chiller — the refrigerant recharge and startup. On a mechanical room replacement this is usually coordinated as part of a broader plant relocation rather than treated as a standalone lift.
