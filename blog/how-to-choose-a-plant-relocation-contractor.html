How to Choose a Plant Relocation Contractor

The pitch decks all look the same. Here is what actually tells you whether a contractor has done this before, and who is supposed to be doing what.

FIELD GUIDE BY THE CREW ✓
REAL LOADS — REAL NUMBERS NO FLUFF, JUST SPECS FROM THE DISPATCH DESK

Plant Relocation · By the Badass Logistics crew · September 21, 2026

// quick answer

Choose a plant relocation contractor by checking who actually owns the sequence — not just the lifting. Ask for a reference move of similar scale, a written asset-tagging and sequencing method, and a clear split of responsibility between the OEM, the millwright, and the rigging crew. A contractor who cannot describe how they hand off between those three roles has not run enough of these to be trusted with yours.

Most plant relocation pitches sound identical: experienced crews, careful planning, minimal downtime. None of that tells you anything, because every contractor says it and almost none of them define it. The differences that actually matter show up in three places — whether the contractor can describe a sequencing method instead of a slogan, whether they can name who else is touching the job besides them, and whether their track record is a real reference you can call or a client logo on a slide.

What actually separates plant relocation contractors?

Sequencing discipline separates them, not lifting capability. Almost any established rigging outfit can pick a 30,000-lb press off a foundation and set it on a trailer — that part of the industry is mature and commoditized. What is rare is a contractor who can walk into your facility, build a survey and asset list before a single machine moves, sequence that list backward from your production restart date, and hold that sequence when the schedule gets pressured halfway through. A relocation with forty machines and a two-week downtime window is not forty rigging jobs. It is one sequencing problem with forty rigging jobs inside it, and the contractors worth hiring talk about the sequencing first.

A second, quieter differentiator is whether the contractor treats your relocation as a single project or as a lifting service bolted onto somebody else's freight and somebody else's installation. If the rigging crew shows up, loads the truck, and considers the job done at the dock door, you have bought a rigging service. If the same team is accountable for the load arriving in install order at the new site and is still on the phone when the millwright hits a leveling problem, you have bought a relocation.

What questions should you ask a plant relocation contractor?

Ask these before you ask for a number, because the number is meaningless until you know what it includes:

  • "Walk me through how you'd sequence this move." A real answer starts with the production restart date and works backward. A weak answer starts with "we'd survey the site" and stops there — surveying is table stakes, sequencing is the skill.
  • "Who tags and tracks the asset list, and in what format?" You want a specific answer — a spreadsheet or system with weight, dimensions, utility requirements and destination mapped for every asset — not "we keep good notes."
  • "What's your split with the OEM and our maintenance team on disconnect and reconnect?" See the next section. A contractor who claims to handle everything, including OEM-only recommissioning, is either wrong or planning to sub it out without telling you.
  • "Give me a reference move of similar size and industry." Not a case study PDF — a name and a number you can call. If they can't produce one inside a day, that tells you something.
  • "What happens if a machine doesn't fit the planned route?" Every real relocation contractor has a story about a doorway that was two inches too narrow. The answer reveals whether they actually walked your building or priced it off a floor plan.
  • "How do you handle a schedule slip mid-project?" Trucking and site conditions change. You want to hear about buffer and phasing, not a shrug.

What does a real track record look like?

A real track record is verifiable, specific, and scaled to your job — not a wall of logos. Three things to check, in order of how hard they are to fake:

  1. Referenceable contacts, not case studies. A case study is marketing copy the contractor wrote about themselves. A reference is a plant manager or facilities director who will get on the phone and tell you what actually went wrong and how the contractor handled it. Ask for two or three, from moves in the last couple of years, and actually call them.
  2. Scale that matches your job. A contractor whose reference moves are all single-machine installs has not necessarily run a forty-asset, multi-phase relocation, even if they're good at what they do. Match the reference to your scope — number of assets, downtime tolerance, and industry — not just to the contractor's overall size.
  3. How they talk about what went wrong. Every relocation of any size has a moment that didn't go to plan — a floor rated wrong, a utility not live on schedule, a dimension that didn't match the drawing. A contractor with a real track record has these stories and can tell you how the plan absorbed the problem. A contractor who insists nothing has ever gone wrong is either inexperienced or not being straight with you.

Track record is also a question of continuity. Ask who is actually project-managing your move, day to day — the same person who sat in the sales meeting, or a name you haven't met yet. A relocation that changes hands between a sales team and an operations team with no overlap is where sequencing plans get lost.

Who owns what between the OEM, the millwright, and the rigger?

These are three different jobs, and a relocation goes wrong most often at the seam between two of them, not inside any one of them. Get this split in writing before anyone signs a contract:

  • The OEM (or authorized service organization). Owns anything that affects the machine's warranty or calibration — de-installation of internal components, controls, and the software or calibration work that has to happen before the machine can run again. On CNC equipment, medical imaging, and anything with a warranty tied to who touched it, this party's sign-off is not optional and their schedule usually sets the date for that machine.
  • The millwright. Owns the mechanical installation at the new site — setting the machine on its pad or foundation, anchoring, leveling to the manufacturer's spec, and aligning it so it holds tolerance once it's running. On precision equipment this is a distinct trade from rigging, even though the same crew sometimes does both. See millwright services for what that scope actually covers.
  • The rigger. Owns the machine from the moment it's free of its foundation to the moment it's set down at the other end — lifting, skating, loading, and the physical transport in between. The rigging crew is not qualified to reprogram a control system, and the OEM's field service technician is rarely equipped to skate a five-ton machine down a corridor. Neither should be doing the other's job.

The failure mode to watch for: a machine sits half-disconnected because the OEM tech hasn't arrived, while the rigging crew is idle and billing. Or the reverse — the machine is rigged out and shipped before the OEM has signed off on de-installation, voiding a warranty nobody meant to void. Either one is a sequencing failure, and it is exactly the kind of gap a contractor who "owns the whole project" is supposed to close by coordinating all three parties against one schedule, rather than assuming the other trades will sort themselves out.

Is the cheapest bid usually the wrong choice?

Usually, yes, because the number that's easy to compare — dollars per load, or a flat rig-and-haul rate — is rarely the number that determines what the move actually costs you. A bid that's missing sequencing, survey time, or coordination with the OEM and millwright often looks lower because it's a smaller scope, not because it's more efficient. The real cost of a relocation is production downtime, and downtime is driven by sequencing quality, not by the line-item rate for lifting one machine. A contractor who has priced in the survey, the asset tagging, and the coordination across trades will usually look more expensive on paper than one who hasn't — and will usually finish closer to the schedule they promised.

Should one contractor manage the whole relocation?

It should be one accountable party for the sequence, even where different trades do the work. You don't need one company physically performing every task — the OEM will always be a separate party on most equipment, and that's normal. What you need is one contractor who is contractually and practically responsible for holding the sequence together: they know when the OEM's tech is scheduled, they know when the new floor's utilities go live, and they build the freight and rigging schedule around both. When responsibility for the sequence is split — say, a freight broker booking trucks with no visibility into the rigging schedule, and a separate rigging crew with no visibility into freight — the gaps between them become your problem on move day, not theirs.

Before you sign, get these in writing

  • A sequencing plan built backward from your production restart date, not just a survey
  • A named asset list format — weight, dimensions, utilities, destination — for every machine
  • A written split of responsibility across OEM, millwright, and rigging crew
  • Two or three referenceable contacts from moves of similar scale, not case studies
  • One point of contact accountable for the whole sequence, named by role
  • How schedule slippage gets handled, agreed before it happens

Bottom line

  • Sequencing discipline, not lifting capability, is what separates plant relocation contractors.
  • Ask for a real reference of similar scale and call it — case studies are marketing, references are verifiable.
  • OEM, millwright, and rigger own three different jobs; get the split in writing before anyone signs.
  • The cheapest bid is often a smaller scope, not a more efficient one.
  • One party needs to be accountable for the whole sequence, even when several trades do the work.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most important factor in choosing a plant relocation contractor?
Sequencing discipline — the ability to build and hold a schedule based on your production restart date, not just lift and haul individual machines. Most established rigging outfits can handle the physical lifting; the ones worth hiring can also describe how they sequence forty machines against a two-week downtime window.
What questions should you ask a plant relocation company before hiring them?
Ask them to walk through how they would sequence your specific move, how they track the asset list, how responsibility splits between the OEM, millwright and rigging crew, and for two or three referenceable contacts from moves of similar scale that you can call directly.
Who is responsible for disconnecting and reconnecting machines during a plant relocation?
Usually the OEM or an authorized service organization, because warranty and calibration depend on it. The rigging crew handles the machine from the moment it is free of its foundation through transport, and the millwright handles setting, anchoring and leveling at the new site. A contractor managing the relocation should coordinate all three against one schedule.
Is the lowest bid usually the best plant relocation contractor?
Not usually. A low bid often reflects a smaller scope — missing survey time, sequencing work, or coordination with the OEM and millwright — rather than a more efficient price. Downtime, which is driven by sequencing quality, is typically the larger real cost, not the per-load rigging rate.
Should the same company handle rigging, freight, and installation for a plant move?
One party should be accountable for the sequence across all three, even if different trades do the physical work. The risk is not having multiple trades involved — that is normal — it is having no single party responsible for the handoffs between them.
How do you verify a plant relocation contractor's track record?
Ask for referenceable contacts from moves of similar scale and industry, then call them, rather than relying on case studies the contractor wrote about itself. Also ask how they talk about what went wrong on past jobs — a contractor with real experience has specific stories about problems and how the plan absorbed them.
