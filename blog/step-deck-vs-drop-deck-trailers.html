Step Deck vs Drop Deck Trailers: Same Trailer, Explained

Two names, one trailer — and a set of deck-height numbers that decide what freight a fleet can actually book without a permit.

FIELD GUIDE BY THE CREW ✓
REAL LOADS — REAL NUMBERS NO FLUFF, JUST SPECS FROM THE DISPATCH DESK

Dispatch · By the Badass Logistics crew · September 21, 2026

// quick answer

A step deck and a drop deck are the same trailer — a two-level flatbed with a low rear deck — the name just changes by region. The lower deck sits roughly 34–42 inches off the ground versus about 5 feet on a flatbed, letting a load run about a foot taller and stay under the common 13′06" height cap. Taller, shorter freight needs a double drop or lowboy instead.

Every dispatch desk gets this question from a driver or a new fleet owner within the first month: "the load board says step deck, the broker said drop deck — do I need a different trailer?" No. It's the same piece of equipment. What actually matters is the two deck heights, what they buy a fleet in legal load height, and the point where that trailer runs out of room and a lowboy has to take the load instead.

What is a step deck trailer?

A step deck trailer is a flatbed built with two deck levels instead of one: a short upper deck sitting over the gooseneck, and a longer lower deck that drops down right behind the tractor and runs to the rear axles. The "step" is the vertical drop between those two levels, usually right behind the kingpin area. That drop is the whole point of the trailer — it buys extra legal height for anything loaded on the lower deck, without needing an oversize-height permit.

A standard flatbed has one flat deck the full length of the trailer, sitting about five feet off the ground. A step deck trades a few feet of that upper deck for a lower rear deck, so cargo that would poke over the legal height limit on a flatbed fits under it on a step deck.

Step deck vs drop deck — what's the actual difference?

None, for nearly every practical purpose. Step deck and drop deck describe the same trailer — a two-tier flatbed with a raised front deck and a dropped rear deck. "Step deck" is the more common term in the Midwest and on most load boards; "drop deck" shows up more in the Southeast and among some manufacturers. A fleet running one is running the other. If a shipper's spec sheet says "drop deck required" and the truck on the yard is tagged step deck, that's not a mismatch to sort out — it's the same trailer.

Where people do draw a line, it's a design detail, not a different trailer class: some builders use "step deck" for a design with a shorter, steeper drop right behind the neck, and "drop deck" for one where the deck lowers more gradually. That distinction shows up in a spec sheet, never in how a dispatcher should route freight. Book by deck height and deck length, not by which of the two names is on the load.

Step deck trailer dimensions

Numbers vary a little by manufacturer, but a fleet planning freight around a step deck should work from these:

Typical step deck specs

  • Overall length — 48 or 53 feet, the two common lengths on the road.
  • Upper deck — about 10 to 11 feet long, sitting roughly 58–62 inches (about 5 feet) off the ground — close to flatbed height.
  • Lower deck — about 37 to 43 feet long on a 48-footer, sitting roughly 34 to 42 inches off the ground depending on the trailer.
  • Width — 102 inches (8.5 feet), same as a standard flatbed.
  • Legal freight weight — typically up to around 48,000 lbs of cargo on the trailer, though the real number is set by axle spacing and state bridge law, not a single figure.

The upper deck is short and mostly used for lighter, shorter freight or a second piece stacked ahead of a taller load on the lower deck. Almost everything that makes a step deck worth booking rides on that lower deck.

What is the ground clearance of a step deck trailer?

Lower than a flatbed, and that's a real operational limit, not just a spec-sheet number. The lower deck sits close enough to the ground that a step deck can drag or high-center where a flatbed wouldn't: steep driveway entrances, humped railroad crossings, and the transition ramps into some loading docks. The trailer's breakover angle — how sharp a crown or dip it can cross before the belly of the lower deck contacts the ground — is tighter than a single-height flatbed's, because there's less distance between the deck and the pavement in the middle of the trailer.

This matters most at the two ends of a run: a driveway apron cut for pickups and vans, a rail spur crossing at an angle instead of square, or a dock ramp built for straight flatbeds. A dispatcher routing a step deck onto an unfamiliar site should ask about grade changes at the gate before the truck shows up empty-handed at a driveway it can't clear. It rarely comes up on interstate miles — it comes up in the last quarter mile.

How high can you load a step deck trailer?

Load height is deck height plus cargo height, checked against the legal limit for the roads the load will run. Most states cap total vehicle height at 13′06" without an oversize permit — that's the standard on the Interstate System and the number most Eastern states hold to. A number of Western states allow 14′0" or more on posted routes (Colorado runs up to 14′6" in places), so the ceiling isn't identical everywhere a load travels.

Working from the common 13′06" cap:

  • Flatbed at a 5-foot deck height leaves about 8′06" of legal cargo height.
  • Step deck at a roughly 40-inch lower deck leaves about 10 feet of legal cargo height — sometimes closer to 10′08" on trailers with a lower deck near 34 inches.

That extra 18 to 24 inches is the entire commercial reason a step deck exists. It's the difference between a piece of equipment needing a height permit and running down the highway on a standard load. A dispatcher should always confirm the actual deck height of the specific trailer being assigned — a couple of inches of variance between manufacturers is the difference between a load that runs legal and one that doesn't.

What is a double drop trailer?

A double drop trailer — also called a lowboy — drops twice instead of once: up over the gooseneck, down into a low center "well," then back up over the rear axles. That well is the lowest point on the trailer, typically sitting somewhere around 18 to 24 inches off the ground, well below a step deck's lower deck. Under the same 13′06" cap, that well can carry cargo roughly 11′06" to 12′00" tall and still run legal height.

The tradeoff is deck length. A step deck's lower deck runs the better part of 40 feet; a double drop's well is much shorter — often somewhere in the 24 to 29-foot range — because the deck has to climb back up over the rear axles. It's built for freight that's tall and dense, not long.

Inside that category, "lowboy" and "RGN" (removable gooseneck) get used almost interchangeably, but the real distinction is how the load gets on. A fixed-neck lowboy loads from the rear, same as a step deck. An RGN detaches its gooseneck and lowers to the ground so equipment can drive straight onto the deck — no ramps, no crane, no forklift required for anything that rolls under its own power.

Step deck vs lowboy — which one does a fleet actually need?

The decision usually comes down to one measurement: how tall is the freight, and does it need to roll on under its own power.

Quick read

  • Run a step deck when: the load is long, moderately tall, and fits inside roughly 10 feet without a permit — machinery, building materials, crated freight, most palletized or crane-loaded equipment.
  • Run a double drop / lowboy when: the load is short but tall and would need an oversize-height permit on a step deck, or when equipment needs to be driven on rather than lifted or ramped.
  • Run a standard flatbed when: the load is under about 8 feet tall and doesn't benefit from either drop — no reason to give up deck length for height nobody needs.

A fleet stocked with a mix of flatbeds, step decks, and a double drop or two can cover most industrial freight without ever touching an oversize-height permit. A fleet running flatbeds only turns down freight a step deck would have carried legally — and that's freight left on the table, not a safety call.

What kind of freight does a step deck actually book?

Equipment and materials that are tall for their length: construction machinery, agricultural equipment, palletized building products stacked higher than a flatbed allows, crated industrial machinery, and pipe or steel loaded in tiered stacks. It's also a common choice any time a load needs to be forklifted or craned on rather than driven, since the lower deck brings the load surface closer to ground-loading equipment than a full-height flatbed does.

It is not the trailer for freight that needs weather protection — that's a Conestoga or enclosed van's job — and it's not built for loads that are both very tall and short in length, which is where a double drop earns its keep instead.

What this means for dispatch

A fleet running mixed freight needs more than one deck type on the yard, and the desk booking loads needs to know the real number on each trailer, not the textbook figure. Two step decks from two different manufacturers can differ by several inches on lower deck height — a piece that clears at 13′04" on a 34-inch deck is over the line on a 42-inch one. We dispatch fleets running flatbed, step deck, reefer, and Conestoga equipment, matching the trailer on the yard to the freight that's actually being offered rather than assuming every step deck in the fleet measures the same.

That's also where equipment mix pays off on a dedicated lane. A lane running the same shipper's freight week after week is worth planning around the trailer that clears every load without a special routing call — sometimes that's the step deck already on the yard, sometimes it's worth adding a double drop to stop turning down the tall, short freight that keeps showing up.

Bottom line

  • Step deck and drop deck are the same trailer under two regional names — book by deck height, not by which name is on the load.
  • A step deck's lower deck (roughly 34–42") buys about a foot of extra legal cargo height over a flatbed's 5-foot deck.
  • A double drop or lowboy's center well (roughly 18–24") buys several feet more, on a much shorter deck.
  • Confirm the actual deck height on the specific trailer being dispatched — manufacturer variance is the difference between legal and permitted.

Frequently asked questions

Is a step deck the same as a drop deck trailer?
Yes, for nearly every practical purpose. Both terms describe a two-level flatbed with a raised front deck and a lower rear deck. "Step deck" is more common in the Midwest, "drop deck" more common in the Southeast and among some manufacturers, but they refer to the same trailer.
What is a step deck trailer used for?
Freight that is too tall for a standard flatbed to carry legally but not so tall it needs a double drop — machinery, building materials, crated equipment, and tiered stacks of pipe or steel, typically loaded by forklift or crane.
How tall can a load be on a step deck trailer?
With a lower deck around 34 to 42 inches and the common 13′06" legal height cap, cargo can usually run close to 10 feet tall without an oversize-height permit. The exact number depends on the specific trailer's deck height and the state the load runs through.
What is the difference between a step deck and a lowboy?
A step deck drops once, to a lower deck around 34–42 inches, running nearly 40 feet long. A lowboy (double drop) drops twice, to a center well around 18–24 inches, but that well is much shorter — often 24 to 29 feet. Lowboys carry taller, shorter freight; step decks carry longer freight with moderate height.
Why does a step deck have less ground clearance than a flatbed?
The lower deck sits closer to the ground by design, which is what gives it extra legal load height. The tradeoff is a tighter breakover angle, so steep driveway aprons, humped rail crossings, and some dock ramps need more care than they would with a full-height flatbed.
What is a double drop trailer?
A trailer that drops twice — up over the gooseneck, down into a low center well, then up again over the rear axles. The well is the lowest point on the trailer, typically 18 to 24 inches off the ground, which lets it carry freight taller than a step deck can legally carry.
