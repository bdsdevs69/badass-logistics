How to Move an Autoclave: Pressure Vessel, Steam & Reinstall

An autoclave is a plumbed pressure vessel wearing a lab-equipment costume. Here is the order the work runs, from the nameplate to the first passed biological indicator.

Medical Rigging · By the Badass Logistics crew · September 27, 2026

// quick answer

Moving an autoclave means moving a code-stamped pressure vessel, not a cabinet. The sequence is: pull the ASME nameplate and National Board data before scheduling, isolate and drain the steam and tempered-drain lines, rig the vessel out of its recess or pit, re-level and re-square the door and chamber at the new site, then run physical qualification and biological-indicator testing before the first load goes through.

An autoclave gets filed under "lab equipment" on most moving quotes, and that is the first mistake. A steam sterilizer of any real size is a code-stamped pressure vessel plumbed into house steam, a tempered drain, water and sometimes compressed air, frequently built into a wall between the dirty and clean sides of a sterile processing department, and it cannot go back into service until someone runs biological indicators through it and gets a pass. The rigging is the smallest part of the job. The paperwork, the utility disconnect, and the requalification are what actually set the schedule.

What makes moving an autoclave different from other lab equipment?

Three things a bench centrifuge or an incubator does not have: a pressure vessel data plate, plumbed utilities that carry live steam at temperature, and a chamber-and-door assembly that has to stay square to seal. A small benchtop sterilizer in a research lab is close to a normal equipment move — unplug it, wheel it, plug it back in. A hospital or CMO floor-loading sterilizer, or a pass-through unit built into a sterile processing wall, is a different job entirely, closer in kind to moving a small boiler than to moving a freezer.

The size split matters because it decides who has to be in the room. Below roughly small chamber volumes, an OEM field tech and a rigging crew can usually turn the job in a shift. Above that — the double-door pass-through units common in hospital sterile processing — the unit is often wider than the corridor doors it has to travel through, was frequently set before the surrounding wall was finished, and comes apart into jacket, chamber, door frames, control cabinet and base skid before anything moves.

What paperwork does a pressure vessel need before it moves?

Pull the ASME nameplate and the National Board registration number before you schedule anything, not after. Most autoclave chambers above a modest size are built to ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section VIII and carry a "U" stamp, and that vessel is registered with the National Board of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Inspectors under a number that follows the vessel, not the building. Moving it does not erase that history, but most states and provinces run their own pressure vessel programs on top of the National Board registration, and many require the new location to be reported and the vessel re-inspected by an authorized inspector before it returns to service — the same logic that applies to relocating a boiler.

Two more items belong on the same list. The pressure relief valve on the jacket or chamber typically needs its own inspection or recertification after handling, since a PRV that has been jarred or repositioned is not assumed good just because the vessel is. And if the unit is due for its periodic hydrostatic or code inspection soon anyway, moving it is the cheap moment to get that done before it goes back into service rather than scheduling a second outage later.

What to pull before scheduling the move

  • ASME nameplate data — vessel serial number, MAWP, year built
  • National Board registration number
  • Whether the destination jurisdiction requires re-registration or re-inspection at the new address
  • Pressure relief valve service records and next-due date
  • OEM service history and any open service bulletins on the unit
  • Whether the destination has a matching pit or wall opening, or one has to be built

How are the steam and drain lines disconnected for a sterilizer move?

Isolated, cooled, drained and locked out — in that order, and never by cutting a live line. A floor-loading autoclave typically has four or five separate connections: house steam or an integral electric steam generator, a jacket steam line separate from the chamber steam line on most models, a water supply, compressed air for the door and valves, and a drain. None of it gets touched until the steam supply is isolated at the source valve, the lines have been allowed to cool and depressurize, and condensate has been bled off — a jacket that still holds pressure is not a rigging problem, it is a burn hazard.

The drain line deserves its own line item because it is the one people forget is not a normal drain. Steam sterilizer discharge leaves the chamber well above the temperature most plumbing codes allow into a sanitary sewer, so the unit typically feeds a tempering assembly that mixes the hot condensate with cold water before it goes downstream. That tempering unit is plumbed near the sterilizer, not inside it, and on a relocation it either moves with the vessel or a new one gets built at the destination — leaving it out is the kind of gap that gets caught by an inspector, not by the rigging crew.

OEM techs handle the actual utility break and the sequence for separating the jacket from the chamber shell where the model requires it; the facility's plumbing and steam trades own everything back to the building's isolation valves. Writing that split down before the outage starts is what keeps a Saturday shutdown from turning into a Sunday one.

What changes when the autoclave is recessed or pit-mounted?

The building has to give the vessel a home before it can leave the old one. Two setups show up constantly in sterile processing and central supply departments, and they are not the same problem. A recessed or pass-through sterilizer is built into a wall so one door opens to the dirty side and the other to the clean side — moving it usually means opening framing on one or both sides, because the unit went in before the wall was finished the same way an MRI magnet often does, and the drywall and door casing at the destination has to be built to receive it before rig-in day.

A pit-mounted sterilizer sits low in a formed recess in the floor slab so the chamber's loading height lines up with the sterile processing carts that roll up to it — without the pit, the door sits too high for the cart-transfer system to work. If the destination room does not already have a matching pit poured to the OEM's dimensions, that is a concrete and structural scope item that has to be scheduled and cured well ahead of the rigging date, not discovered on move day. Confirming pit depth, width and drain tie-in against the actual floor plan — not the equipment manual's generic dimensions — is a site-walk item, the same way maze geometry is on a linac move.

How do you keep the door and chamber square after relocation?

By leveling and shimming the frame to a tolerance most equipment does not need, because a sterilizer door seals on a gasket rather than a latch. The chamber and door assembly are built and machined as a matched pair at the factory. If the base frame racks even slightly out of level when it is set — on an uneven pit floor, an unshimmed leg, or a base plate that was not fully supported — the door can bind against its guide rails or fail to seat evenly on the gasket, and a sterilizer that leaks steam at the door fails its own safety interlocks before it fails a validation test.

Reinstall crews check level and square with a precision level and a square across the door opening, not by eye, and shim the base frame until both read true before a single utility connection is made. On pass-through units, the two door frames also have to line up with the finished wall openings on both the clean and dirty sides, which means the wall framing crew and the rigging crew are working the same opening from opposite ends of the schedule and need the same set of dimensions.

What re-validation is required before a relocated sterilizer treats another load?

A full requalification, not a plug-in-and-run test. Moving a sterilizer counts as a significant change under the standards hospital sterile processing departments follow, which means it needs the same qualification work as a new installation: physical qualification of the steam supply, temperature and pressure distribution through an empty chamber over multiple cycles, and then biological indicator testing — spore strips run through representative loads, incubated, and read as a pass before the unit is released for clinical or lab use. Vacuum-assisted units also get a Bowie-Dick test to confirm air removal is working, since a leak that a temperature chart will not catch shows up there first.

None of that testing belongs to the rigging crew. It is run by the facility's sterile processing or quality team, or by the OEM's field service group, and it is the item most often left off the schedule because everyone assumes the machine "just needs setting up." Build the qualification runs into the outage plan from day one — they typically add a day or more after reinstall before the room is back in production, and a department that plans the move around rigging alone will find the room still closed on the day it expected to reopen.

Who de-installs and reinstalls a hospital or lab autoclave?

The OEM or an OEM-certified service provider handles anything that touches the pressure vessel itself — breaking the jacket and chamber steam connections, separating the unit into its shippable sections, and the final leveling, connection and start-up on the other end — because the manufacturer's warranty and the code compliance both depend on that sequence being followed exactly. The facility's steam, plumbing and electrical trades own the building side of every connection back to its isolation point. The rigging crew owns the vessel from the moment it is disconnected and free to move to the moment it is set, leveled and squared in its new pit or recess.

That three-way split is worth confirming in writing before the first shift, the same way it matters on any machinery move that involves an OEM and a live utility. The failure mode is always the same: everyone assumes someone else is draining the jacket, or capping the drain, or building the pit, and it surfaces at the worst possible hour of a weekend shutdown.

Bottom line

  • An autoclave above bench size is a code-stamped pressure vessel first and a piece of lab equipment second.
  • Pull the ASME nameplate and National Board number early — the destination jurisdiction may require re-registration or re-inspection.
  • Steam, jacket and drain lines get isolated and cooled before disconnect; the tempered-drain assembly is part of the scope, not an afterthought.
  • Pit-mounted and pass-through units need a matching opening or pit built at the destination before rig-in day.
  • Level and square the base to the tolerance the door gasket needs — it seals on precision, not on a latch.
  • Physical qualification and biological-indicator testing come after reinstall and belong on the schedule, not as an assumed formality.

Frequently asked questions

Is an autoclave a pressure vessel that needs special paperwork to move?
Above bench size, yes. Most chamber and jacket assemblies are built to ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section VIII and registered with the National Board under a number that follows the vessel. Many jurisdictions require the new location to be reported and the vessel re-inspected by an authorized inspector before it returns to service, so pull the nameplate data and registration number before scheduling the move rather than after.
How do you disconnect an autoclave for a move?
Isolate the steam supply at the building valve, let the lines cool and depressurize, bleed off condensate, and only then break the jacket steam, chamber steam, water, air and drain connections. OEM techs handle the vessel-side disconnection and the sequence for separating jacket from chamber; facility trades own everything back to the isolation point.
What is different about moving a pit-mounted or pass-through sterilizer?
The destination needs the same infrastructure the origin had before the unit can be set. Pit-mounted units need a floor pit poured to the OEM's dimensions so cart-loading height lines up correctly, and pass-through units built into a sterile processing wall need matching framing and door openings on both the clean and dirty sides. Both are structural or framing scope that has to be scheduled ahead of rig-in day, not discovered on it.
Why does an autoclave need to be leveled so precisely after a move?
The door seals against the chamber on a gasket, and the door and chamber were machined as a matched pair. If the base frame is even slightly out of level, the door can bind on its guide rails or fail to seat evenly, and a sterilizer that leaks steam at the door will fail its own safety interlocks before it ever reaches a validation test. Reinstall crews check level and square with instruments and shim the frame before any utility is reconnected.
What testing does a relocated autoclave need before it can be used again?
A full requalification: physical qualification of steam supply and temperature distribution through empty cycles, then biological indicator testing with spore strips run through representative loads, incubated and passed. Vacuum-assisted units also get a Bowie-Dick test for air removal. This is run by the facility's sterile processing or quality team or the OEM, and it typically adds a day or more after reinstall before the room is back in service.
Who is responsible for the drain line on a sterilizer relocation?
It depends on where the connection sits, but it is rarely a simple drain. Sterilizer discharge is hot enough that most plumbing codes require it to pass through a tempering assembly that mixes it with cold water before it reaches the sanitary sewer. That assembly either moves with the vessel or has to be built new at the destination, and leaving it off the scope is a common gap between an OEM quote and a rigging quote.
