Project Freight & Project Cargo

Project cargo is freight tied to one industrial project instead of one shipment: machines leaving one plant for another, new equipment landing by container, crated parts that have to arrive in install order. We run that freight — sometimes called project freight, sometimes project cargo — as part of the project, planned with the crew that rigs it and on schedule with the crew that sets it.

Quick answer: Project freight — also called project cargo — is any shipment that belongs to one industrial project instead of a single order: a plant-to-plant machine move, a container of new equipment landing from a factory overseas, or crated parts that have to arrive in the order they get installed. What separates it from ordinary freight is the plan: weights, dimensions, packing lists, and delivery windows are built around an install schedule, not a shipping date. Badass Logistics runs that plan for the jobs we rig — container receiving, crating and export prep, staged multi-load sequencing, and FTL or dedicated capacity through our licensed broker and carrier partners, timed to our rigging crews. We handle project-based moves, not one-off loads.

Freight that belongs to a project

Most industrial projects split the work three ways: a rigger on each end, a trucking company in the middle, and a warehouse somewhere that nobody visits. Each one does its piece. Nobody owns the sequence. So the truck shows up before the pad is poured, the machine that was supposed to go in first arrives last, and a crew stands around on the clock waiting for a load that's sitting at a dock two states away.

We started running freight because our rigging jobs kept needing it done right. Badass Logistics plans the freight with the rigging — which pieces move first, how they're packed, what equipment they ride on, and exactly when they need to land so the crew is ready to set them. One project team, one schedule, one point of contact from the first container to the last machine.

That also means we're clear about what we don't do. We don't sell one-off spot loads off a board. Freight comes with a project — a plant relocation, a production line install, an imported equipment package, a build-out receiving deliveries in phases. Transport runs on our network of licensed broker and carrier partners, coordinated inside the project plan.

project freight vs. ordinary freight

What makes a load "project freight" instead of ordinary freight?

Ordinary freight is one shipment: one bill of lading, one truck, a pickup and delivery date that don't depend on anything else. Project freight is freight that can't be booked that way, because it's only one piece of a larger move.

  • It's tied to a sequence — a piece has to arrive before or after another piece, not just "sometime this week"
  • It spans multiple loads that all have to land on a plan, not a single pickup
  • It carries equipment, not boxes — machines that need crating, draining, or specific trailer types
  • It has a receiving or install crew waiting on the other end, so a missed window costs labor, not just a delivery fee
  • It often starts at a port or rail ramp with a container, not a dock door

A single machine going from a dealer to a shop floor is ordinary freight. A production line landing in six containers, staged, crated, and delivered in install order over three weeks is project freight — the version we run.

two common triggers

Plant-to-plant moves and inbound new equipment

Project freight almost always starts one of two ways: equipment that already exists moving between facilities, or new equipment arriving from a manufacturer or overseas for the first time. Both need the same thing — a plan built around the install, not the shipping date.

Plant-to-plant moves

A line, cell, or department moving from one facility to another — a consolidation, a lease ending, a plant closing. Machines get rigged out, freighted to the new site, and rigged back in on the same schedule, so equipment isn't sitting on a trailer any longer than it has to.

Inbound new-equipment deliveries

New machines arriving from a factory or importer, often by container, sometimes as a single oversized crate. We schedule receiving against the install date, not the ship date, so equipment isn't staged in a warehouse for months waiting on a foundation that isn't poured yet.

a typical project

From the container to the foundation

Here's what project freight looks like on a common job: a manufacturer importing a new production line that has to be installed in a plant that's still running.

1. The containers land

Containers arrive at the port or rail ramp over a few weeks. We schedule pickups against free time and dray them to a warehouse or staging site, where heavy components are rigged out of the box instead of forked out blind.

2. Inspect, stage, prep

Every piece is checked against the packing list, photographed, and staged by install sequence. Anything that needs crating or re-packing for the final leg gets done here.

3. Deliver in order

Loads move to the plant on dedicated capacity, in the order the install plan calls for, on equipment matched to the freight — air-ride for sensitive machines, open deck for crane-loaded pieces.

4. Rig and set

Our crew is on site when each truck arrives. The machine comes off the truck, goes through the door, and is set and leveled on its foundation. Same team from the port to the pad.

why one team

Why does project freight need one team instead of three vendors?

When freight is bought separately from rigging, it gets bought on price and timed to the carrier's schedule. When it's planned with the rigging, it's timed to the job. That difference shows up in the places projects actually lose money:

  • Crews waiting on trucks, or trucks waiting on crews
  • Containers sitting past free time because nobody was ready to receive them
  • Machines arriving out of sequence and double-handled on site
  • Damage from packing and securement nobody checked
  • Nobody accountable when something goes wrong between vendors

One project team doesn't make freight free of problems. It makes someone responsible for solving them before they cost the job a day. That's the hand-off a rigger, a carrier, and a warehouse never fix on their own — the plan has to belong to one team, not pass between three.

paperwork that keeps a project moving

What documentation does a project freight move need?

A project move generates more paperwork than a single shipment, and one missing piece can hold up a whole load. We keep it with the freight plan, not scattered across separate vendors:

  • A packing list for every container and crate, checked against what actually shows up
  • A bill of lading and sequence number for each load, tied to the install order
  • Commercial invoices and country-of-origin paperwork that travels with imported equipment
  • Crating and export-prep records for anything that moved by container
  • Delivery photos and a signed proof of delivery for every load, not just the last one

None of that replaces a customs broker or freight forwarder's own paperwork on an import — it's the documentation that keeps the receiving crew, the install schedule, and the freight plan reading from the same page.

Project freight FAQ

What is project freight, and is it the same as project cargo?
Yes — project freight and project cargo are the same thing said two ways: freight planned and managed as part of one project, with containers, crated equipment, machines, and materials moved in a set sequence to a set schedule instead of booked one shipment at a time. See our guide to project cargo.
How is project freight different from regular freight?
Regular freight is a single shipment with its own pickup and delivery date. Project freight is one piece of a larger move — a container, a crate, or a truckload that has to land in sequence with everything else on an install schedule, not on its own timeline.
Do you take single one-off loads?
No. We handle project-based moves: relocations, installs, build-outs, and equipment packages with multiple shipments. We don't sell spot loads.
Do you own the trucks?
No. Transport runs on our network of licensed broker and carrier partners, coordinated inside the project plan. Our own crews handle the rigging, devanning, and setting.
Can you receive containers of imported equipment?
Yes — pickup at port or rail, drayage to a warehouse or staging site, devanning with riggers on the heavy pieces, inspection, and staging. See container to warehouse.
Do you handle plant-to-plant equipment moves?
Yes. Machines coming out of one facility and going into another are freighted on the same schedule they're rigged and relocated on, so equipment isn't sitting on a trailer between the two ends of the move.
What documentation do you handle on a project freight move?
Packing lists checked against every container and crate, bills of lading tied to install sequence, commercial invoices and country-of-origin paperwork on imported equipment, and delivery photos and signed proof of delivery for every load — kept with the freight plan.
What equipment types can you use for project freight?
Whatever the freight calls for — dry van, air-ride, flatbed, step deck, conestoga, and enclosed trailers — matched to each load's weight, dimensions, and how sensitive it is.
Do I need to use your riggers to use project freight?
Project freight works best when it's planned with the rigging, and most of our freight comes out of jobs we rig. If you have your own install team, we'll plan deliveries around their schedule instead.
