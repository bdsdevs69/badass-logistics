Plant & Production Facility Relocation Services

When the whole floor moves, the order is everything. We relocate production lines, machine cells, process equipment, and entire facilities — survey, scope of work, teardown, sequenced freight, reinstall, re-level — planned so your line is down only for the move window, not a day longer.

Quick answer: Plant relocation — also called production facility relocation — is the sequenced move of a manufacturing or process facility with minimal downtime: survey and asset list, a written scope of work, disconnect and teardown, rigging and freight between sites, and reinstall and re-level in the new layout. Badass Logistics runs all of it as one project with one accountable team, not separate rigging, transport, and installation contracts. A plant relocation quote is built from your asset list, both floor plans, the distance between sites, and your production start-up date — send those and we return a written scope of work and quote before anything is scheduled.

One team owns the whole relocation

A plant move isn't one big lift — it's dozens or hundreds of moves in the right order. Disconnect, teardown, crating, freight, reinstall, recommission. Get the sequence wrong and the new floor sits half-built while production bleeds. Badass Logistics runs the entire project with one accountable team, so nothing falls into the cracks between the rigger, the trucking company, and the installer.

That gap is the single biggest reason a production facility relocation runs long. When rigging, freight, and installation are three separate contracts, no one owns the sequence — the rigger stages crates and moves on to the next job, the truck arrives on its own schedule instead of the install crew's, and if a machine shows up damaged or short a part, three vendors point at each other while your line stays down. One scope of work with one contractor removes that handoff entirely.

We map the path of travel and floor loading at both sites, tear down and label, crate and prep what needs it, and run the loads as project freight on dedicated lanes timed to the crew. At the new building our rigging and machinery moving crews rig and set every machine back to spec in the new layout, with millwright reinstall and alignment on the machines that need it — including the precision CNC machines that have to come back holding tolerance.

New to planning a facility move? Start with our plant relocation checklist, then see how we handle the tricky pieces like re-leveling machines to spec at the new site.

the sequence

Planned backward from your start-up date

Every relocation plan we build starts at the end: the day the first line has to run in the new building. From there we work backward — which machines are needed first, which utilities have to be live before they land, which pads and anchors have to be ready, and how many days each teardown and reinstall really takes.

What gets surveyed

Both buildings get walked and measured before anything is scheduled: every machine's weight, footprint, and utility drops; floor loading and slab condition along the path of travel and at the new pad; doorway, aisle, and ceiling clearances; crane hook height and dock access at both ends; and where the new floor plan puts each machine relative to power, air, and process piping. That survey becomes the asset list that drives the rigging plan, the crating plan, and the truck count.

The disconnect and reconnect boundary

Every machine on the asset list gets a clear line drawn around it: what we disconnect and reconnect as part of the move, and what stays with your electricians, controls integrator, or the OEM's own field techs. Mechanical disconnect, rigging, transport, and mechanical reinstall — setting, anchoring, and leveling — run on our schedule. Utility drops, controls tie-ins, and any startup that requires the OEM's own technician are written into the plan up front and sequenced around our crew, not discovered on move day.

Phased move or single shutdown

Most plants can't go dark for the whole move, so we plan phase by phase — often line by line or department by department — so part of production keeps running at the old site while the first lines come up at the new one. A single shutdown, where the whole facility comes down at once, is faster end to end but only works when the production calendar allows it and the receiving building is fully ready on day one. We lay out both options against your asset list and start-up date so the trade-off is a decision, not a guess.

Documentation and labeling discipline

Every cable, hose, guard, and fastener gets tagged and photographed before it comes off, cross-referenced to the machine's position on the new floor plan. Floor plan revisions, the asset list, and the punch list are kept as one live set of documents through the whole project, not separate paperwork per vendor. Reassembly at the new site should be a checklist against that record, not a crew trying to remember how something came apart.

the scope of work

What a production facility relocation service includes

A written scope of work is what separates a plant relocation from three vendors doing their own piece of it. Before a single machine moves, the scope of work lays out the asset list with weights, dimensions, and destinations; the disconnect/reconnect boundary for every machine; the rigging plan and equipment needed at both ends; the freight plan and load sequence; the crating and packaging standard for anything shipped; the phased or single-shutdown schedule tied to your start-up date; and the reinstall and leveling spec each machine has to meet before it's handed back.

That scope is what keeps rigging, freight, and installation from becoming three separate contracts with three separate schedules. When one contractor owns the whole scope, a delay in teardown adjusts the freight schedule automatically instead of leaving trucks waiting or crews standing around a machine that hasn't arrived — and if something needs to be re-sequenced mid-project, there's one plan to change, not three.

the freight in the middle

Where rigging meets project freight

A relocation is the clearest example of why we run freight at all. Between the rig-out and the rig-in, you might have dozens of truckloads of machines, crated parts, racking, and tooling that all need to arrive in a specific order — not whenever a truck happens to be available.

So the freight is planned with the rigging. Loads are built around weight, dimensions, and install order. Equipment types are matched to the freight — air-ride for precision machines, open deck for tall or crane-loaded pieces, enclosed for crated parts. Transport runs through our licensed broker and carrier partners, usually on dedicated lanes for the length of the move, so the same trucks cycle between sites on a schedule the crews can plan around.

For equipment coming in from overseas or out of storage, we handle container-to-warehouse receiving and staging as part of the same project.

multi-site consolidation

When two plants become one

A consolidation is a different planning problem than a single-site relocation. Instead of one asset list moving into one empty building, you're often merging two live production floors — sometimes with overlapping or duplicate equipment — into a receiving building that has to hold both floor plans without exceeding its own floor loading, utility capacity, or aisle widths.

The survey has to answer questions a single relocation doesn't: which duplicate machines actually make the trip and which are decommissioned, how the two production schedules interleave so neither site goes fully dark before the other is ready, and how the combined asset list fits the new building's layout without recreating the bottlenecks either original floor had. We plan consolidations the same way — asset list, scope of work, sequenced rigging and freight, reinstall — but the sequencing has two source floors to reconcile instead of one.

the quote

What a plant relocation quote is built from

A plant relocation quote isn't a per-mile rate — it's a project estimate built from the same information that becomes the scope of work. Send us the asset list (or let us survey and build one), the floor plans for both the current and receiving buildings, the distance between sites, your production start-up date, and whether the move needs to be phased or can run as a single shutdown.

From there the process is straightforward: a survey call or site walk to confirm the asset list and both floor plans, a written scope of work covering the disconnect/reconnect boundary, the rigging and freight plan, and the reinstall spec, then a project quote against that scope — not a rough estimate that changes once the crew is on site.

Plant relocation FAQ

What does a production facility relocation service include?
A survey and asset list, a written scope of work covering the disconnect/reconnect boundary, the rigging plan, the freight plan, and the reinstall spec, then execution as one project: teardown, sequenced freight, and reinstall and re-leveling to spec — run by one contractor instead of separate rigging, transport, and installation vendors.
What information do you need to give a plant relocation quote?
The asset list (or a survey to build one), floor plans for both the current and receiving buildings, the distance between sites, your production start-up date, and whether the move needs to be phased or can run as a single shutdown. That becomes the scope of work the quote is built against.
How do you keep downtime short on a plant move?
The whole project is planned before teardown — path of travel, floor loads, machine sequence, and a phased schedule built around your production calendar, so each line is down only for its move window.
What's the difference between a phased move and a single shutdown?
A phased move relocates lines or departments one at a time so part of production keeps running while the rest transitions. A single shutdown takes the whole facility down at once — faster overall, but only workable when the production calendar allows it and the receiving building is fully ready on day one.
How is a multi-site consolidation different from a single relocation?
Consolidation means reconciling two live production floors — and often duplicate equipment — into one receiving building without exceeding its floor loading, utility capacity, or aisle widths, instead of moving one asset list into an empty building.
Who handles utility disconnect and reconnect?
We draw the boundary on the asset list up front: mechanical disconnect, rigging, transport, and mechanical reinstall run on our schedule, while utility drops, controls tie-ins, and any OEM-required startup are sequenced with your electricians, controls integrator, or the equipment builder's own field techs.
Can you relocate a full production line?
Yes — single lines, machine cells, and complete facilities, including disconnect, labeling, crating, freight, reinstallation, and re-leveling to spec.
How is the freight handled on a plant relocation?
As project freight: loads planned in install order and moved through our licensed broker and carrier partners, usually on dedicated lanes for the length of the move, timed to the rigging crews at both ends.
How long does a plant relocation take?
It depends on the number of machines, the distance, and how much reinstall and utility work the new building needs. We build the timeline from your asset list during the survey.
Do you remove the equipment we're leaving behind?
Yes. Equipment that isn't moving can be rigged out and decommissioned so the old building is ready to hand back.
